Smart Home Digital Twin with Statecharts

A four-ESP32 smart-home testbed wired through Firebase, with statechart-driven control and rolling ML pipelines for fire risk, occupancy, and power. Presented at EDTConf 2025 (ACM/IEEE MODELS 2025 Companion). Live demo available.

§ 01

The problem

Industry "digital twins" of smart homes are mostly dashboards that visualize sensor readings. They're not models in any formal sense, you can't simulate them, you can't verify their behaviour against safety properties, and you definitely can't ask them what would happen if the smoke alarm fired while the garage was open and the HVAC was ramping.

I wanted a twin that was actually executable: a statechart per subsystem, explicit modes and transitions, real ML on top, and a cloud-side view that mirrored the physical system tightly enough to drive decisions instead of just visualizing them.

§ 02

The architecture

Four ESP32 microcontrollers, one per subsystem, talking to each other over an OTA-ready ESP-NOW mesh and syncing state to Firebase Realtime Database. Each subsystem runs its own statechart and emits structured events; a centralized reasoning layer in the cloud subscribes to the event stream and runs rolling ML.

smart-home-twin · subsystems

esp-01 (fire) smoke + temp + CO sensors → fire-risk statechart {SAFE, WATCH, ALARM}

esp-02 (garage) door state + presence → garage statechart {CLOSED, OPENING, OPEN, FAULT}

esp-03 (lighting) presence + ambient lux → lighting statechart {OFF, AUTO, DIM, BRIGHT}

esp-04 (energy) CT clamp + HVAC → energy statechart {IDLE, ACTIVE, PEAK}

────────────────────────────────────────────────

cloud Firebase events → rolling 10-min features → ML reasoning

§ 03

The ML layer

Rolling 10-minute pipelines run in the cloud. Three models cover the three jobs the twin needs to do:

  • Fire risk. Random Forest over smoke, temperature, CO, and historical baselines. Random Forest is the right call here because risk classification needs interpretability and fast inference, not raw accuracy.
  • Occupancy estimate. LSTM over presence detector counts, door events, and lighting state. Short-horizon forecasts feed the HVAC and lighting controllers.
  • Power forecast. LSTM over the energy subsystem's CT-clamp stream with one-hour ahead prediction for load-shifting.

Predictions get pushed back into Firebase as twin attributes, so the physical subsystems can react (e.g., the lighting statechart transitions to AUTO when occupancy ≥ 1).

§ 05

What I learned

The statechart-first approach pays off the moment you have to debug something at 11pm. Every state is explicit, every transition is named, and the "weird" behaviour the twin emits is always a transition you didn't think about, not a tangle of conditional logic you have to grep through.

ML predictions are most useful when they're surfaced as twin attributes, not pushed as alerts. The physical layer can choose what to do with them, and you don't end up with a separate alerting system to babysit.

Firebase is great for state mirroring and terrible for time-series. The right next step is a TSDB beside it for the rolling window features, with Firebase reserved for the twin's "now" view.

§ 06

Where it's going

This twin became the testbed for ArchML4IoT, the architecture DSL that compiles high-level IoT specs into the statecharts you see above. Together they're forming a model-driven workflow where you describe the system once, then generate the runtime artifacts plus the simulation model.
