Federated Anomaly Detection for Smart Cities

A federated learning pipeline that detects anomalies in smart-city IoT telemetry without centralizing raw data. FedProx + transformer-autoencoder backbones, evaluated across heterogeneous (non-IID) clients. First-author paper with Khwaja, Anpalagan, and Baljon, published at WiSPNET 2025 (IEEE).

§ 01

The problem

Smart-city telemetry comes from districts, buildings, and devices that look nothing alike statistically. Centralizing it for training is a privacy non-starter; training one model per client misses the joint signal. Federated learning is the obvious middle path, until you actually try to make it converge on data this heterogeneous.

The question I wanted to answer: how well does federated anomaly detection hold up across realistic non-IID partitions, and what are the right architectural choices for the local model?

§ 02

The approach

Implemented in PyTorch, with two aggregation strategies (FedAvg and FedProx) and two local-model families (transformer encoder + autoencoder reconstruction). Each client owned a partition of smart-city telemetry; partitions were intentionally non-IID by feature distribution and label imbalance.

The autoencoder backbone catches anomalies as reconstruction error spikes; the transformer encoder captures longer temporal dependencies and improves on rare-pattern detection. FedProx's proximal term is what kept the global model from being pulled apart by drifty clients.

Full benchmarks for communication-round count and convergence stability appear in the paper. The headline: FedProx + autoencoder hit 86.43% accuracy on the non-IID partition while staying communication-efficient compared to the transformer variant.

§ 04

What I learned

FedAvg's "just average the weights" intuition stops working the moment client distributions diverge meaningfully. FedProx's proximal regularizer is doing more than the math suggests, it's the difference between models that drift apart and models that share enough structure to be useful.

Autoencoders are unfairly maligned in modern ML discourse. For anomaly detection on telemetry where you have lots of "normal" and very little "weird," reconstruction error is a perfectly reasonable signal and ridiculously cheap to compute compared to the transformer alternative.

The most important plot in the paper isn't the accuracy bar chart. It's the convergence curve across communication rounds: that's what tells you whether the system will work in a real deployment where bandwidth is the constraint.

§ 05

Where it's going

Next iterations: a personalized FedProx variant that lets each client keep a local head adapted to its own distribution, plus a real evaluation of differential-privacy budgets and how much they actually cost detection performance in practice.
